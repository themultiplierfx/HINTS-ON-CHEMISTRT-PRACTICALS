NOTE ON GASES 2

1. LISTEN! The Chemistry of Carbon(IV) Oxide with Lime Water

When carbon(IV) oxide ($\text{CO}_2$) is bubbled into lime water, it forms a milky precipitate:

CO₂ + Ca(OH)₂ → CaCO₃↓ + H₂O     (milky solution)

If the gas is introduced in excess, the milky solution disappears into a completely clear, colourless solution:

CaCO₃ + H₂O + CO₂ → Ca(HCO₃)₂     (colourless solution)

Applying heat directly to this clear liquid decomposes it, forcing the milky solution to return instantly:

Ca(HCO₃)₂ + HEAT → CaCO₃↓ + H₂O + CO₂     (milky solution)
🔬 Chemical Reason: The initial milkiness or white precipitate is caused by Calcium Trioxocarbonate(IV) ($\text{CaCO}_3$), which is completely insoluble in water. Adding excess gas converts this into Calcium Hydrogentrioxocarbonate(IV) ($\text{Ca(HCO}_3)_2$), which is soluble in water. Heating it breaks this down, turning it back into insoluble $\text{CaCO}_3$.

Sources and Applications of Carbon(IV) Oxide

Major Commercial Sources:

  • From industrial fermentation processes as a primary by-product.
  • From blast furnaces, when limestone is heated to make quicklime.

Important Practical Uses of $\text{CO}_2$:

  • Preserves food matrices and acts as an effective refrigerant for perishable goods like ice cream.
  • Gives a crisp, refreshing taste to soft drinks and carbonated beverages.
  • Serves as a thermal coolant loop modifier inside nuclear reactors.
  • Acts as a superior fire extinguisher compared to water for petrol fires because it is less dense than petrol and smothers flames.
  • Causes baking dough to become light and rise perfectly.

Note: Commercial baking powder consists of two major components: $\text{NaHCO}_3$ (Sodium Hydrogentrioxocarbonate(IV)) and tartaric acid.

2. Reactions of Chlorine Gas with Sodium Hydroxide

Chlorine gas ($\text{Cl}_2$) reacts differently depending on the concentration and temperature of the Sodium Hydroxide ($\text{NaOH}$) base:

When passed into dilute aqueous $\text{NaOH}$, it yields a pale yellow substance known as sodium oxochlorate(I):

Cl₂ gas + dil. NaOH → NaOCl + NaCl + H₂O

However, if the $\text{NaOH}$ is concentrated and hot, sodium trioxochlorate(V) is formed instead:

Cl₂ gas + conc. NaOH → NaOclO₃ + NaCl + H₂O

3. Manufacture of Bleaching Powder

Passing chlorine gas over freshly prepared, dry slaked lime ($\text{Ca(OH)}_2$) creates industrial bleaching powder:

Cl₂ gas + freshly prepared Ca(OH)₂ → CaOCl₂.H₂O(s)

Note: $\text{CaOCl}_2\cdot\text{H}_2\text{O}_{(s)}$ represents the active form of standard commercial bleaching powder.
