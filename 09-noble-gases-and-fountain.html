NOTE ON NOBLE GAS & FOUNTAIN EXPERIMENT

1. Practical Study of Noble Gases

The noble gases include helium, neon, argon, krypton, xenon, and radon. They make up roughly 1 percent of atmospheric air.

  • Of all the noble gases, argon is the most abundant in the atmosphere.
  • Argon is used to fill electric lamps because it creates an inert shield that prevents the oxidation of the hot metal lamp filament.
  • Noble gases emit distinct bright coloured lights under reduced pressure conditions and high electrical voltage. This is why they are widely used in commercial advertising signs.
  • Helium is non-combustible and almost as light as hydrogen. This makes it much safer for inflating weather or observation balloons, unlike hydrogen, which is highly flammable.
  • Neon lights are used in aerodrome beacons because they have an exceptionally high power of fog penetration, serving as visible marker targets.

2. The Fountain Experiment Setup

The fountain experiment is a classic laboratory demonstration used to show the extreme solubility of certain gases in water.

  • Gases used for this experiment include $\text{HCl}$, $\text{NO}_2$, and $\text{NH}_3$ because they are extremely soluble in water.
  • When water meets an inverted flask filled with these gases, the gas dissolves so rapidly that it creates a partial vacuum. Atmospheric pressure then forces water up a tube like a fountain.
  • When demonstrating with acidic gases like $\text{HCl}$ or $\text{NO}_2$, the water turns acidic, causing blue litmus indicator components to turn a sharp red fountain color.

3. Thermal Mechanics of Ammonium Salts

Ammonium salts are chemically unstable when exposed to high temperature ranges. They decompose upon heating and, in some cases, undergo **sublimation** directly.

Understanding Sublimation:

Sublimation occurs when a solid substance transitions directly into a gaseous state upon heating without passing through an intermediate liquid state. On cooling, the gas phase desublimes back into a solid lattice format.

  • The best example of an ammonium salt that sublimes is provided by Ammonium Chloride ($\text{NH}_4\text{Cl}$).
  • Another common non-ammonium substance that sublimes easily on heating is iodine crystal.
🔬 Core Classroom Rule: Always remember that Ammonia gas ($\text{NH}_3$) is always evolved whenever an ammonium salt undergoes thermal decomposition on heating in your test tubes!
