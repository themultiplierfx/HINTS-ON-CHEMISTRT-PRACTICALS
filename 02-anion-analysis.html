Chapter 2

Comprehensive Study on Anions

Anions are negative ions attracted to the positive electrode (anode) during electrolysis. They are also called non-metallic ions. Our focus under the syllabus is on identifying five primary anions: Cl⁻, SO₃²⁻, CO₃²⁻, NO₃⁻, and SO₄²⁻.

💡 Memory Anchor Note: Think of the anode and anion like husband and wife—they are bound to look for each other! However, always remember their underlying electrical charges are opposite: While an anode is a positive physical conductor plate, an anion is a negative chemical ion species.

Preliminary Acid Screener Matrix

Adding cold dilute Hydrochloric acid (HCl) or concentrated Sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄) directly to dry solid crystal samples triggers rapid gas evolutions that map out the target chemical groupings immediately.

Target Anion Group Immediate Practical Observation Profile Analytical Inference
Carbonate (CO₃²⁻) Instant, vigorous effervescence occurs. A colourless, odourless gas escapes that turns clear lime water milky. CO₂ gas evolved; CO₃²⁻ present.
Sulphite (SO₃²⁻) Effervescence is observed. Evolved gas has a highly suffocating smell of burning sulphur and turns orange acidified K₂Cr₂O₇ to green. SO₂ gas evolved; SO₃²⁻ present.
Chloride (Cl⁻) Reacts aggressively with concentrated H₂SO₄, yielding pungent white fumes in moist air that turn blue litmus paper red. HCl gas evolved; Cl⁻ indicated.
Nitrate (NO₃⁻) Upon adding concentrated H₂SO₄ and heating gently, dense reddish-brown fumes with an irritating smell escape. Adding copper turnings intensifies the brown fumes. NO₂ gas evolved; NO₃⁻ present.

Confirmatory Assay for Tetraoxosulphate(VI) (SO₄²⁻)

Unlike carbon or sulphur oxides, the stable sulphate ion grouping (SO₄²⁻) will not yield diagnostic gaseous compounds when treated with dilute acids. We confirm its presence using a strict precipitation sequence:

  1. Dissolve a small portion of your sample salt in about 5cm³ of distilled water to form a clear aqueous solution.
  2. Divide the liquid into separate test-tube fractions.
  3. To the first sample fraction, add a few drops of Barium chloride solution (BaCl₂(aq)). A thick, chalky white precipitate forms instantly.
  4. Add excess dilute Hydrochloric acid (HCl) to the mixture.
🔬 Critical Observation Rule: If this thick white precipitate **fails to dissolve** and remains completely insoluble in excess strong acid, your analytical inference is completely locked: **SO₄²⁻ is definitively confirmed**.
