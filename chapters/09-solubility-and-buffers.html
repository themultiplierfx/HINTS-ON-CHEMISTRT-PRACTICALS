Chapter 9

Solubility of Salts & Buffer Solutions

Understanding salt interactions with water solvent frames is critical when preparing liquid testing reagents or tracking chemical equilibrium properties in laboratory assays.

Master Rules for Salt Solubility in Water

During qualitative workflows, you must quickly know whether a precipitate is forming or if a salt completely dissolves. Memorize this checklist rule structure:

Salt Family Class Solubility Behavior Baseline Rules
All Trioxonitrate(V) ($NO_3^-$) Salts All soluble without exception.
All Sodium, Potassium & Ammonium Salts All soluble without exception.
Chlorides ($Cl^-$) All soluble except Lead(II) chloride ($PbCl_2$) and Silver chloride ($AgCl$). ($PbCl_2$ dissolves easily in hot water).
Tetraoxosulphates(VI) ($SO_4^{2-}$) All soluble except Barium sulphate ($BaSO_4$), Lead(II) sulphate ($PbSO_4$), and Calcium sulphate ($CaSO_4$).
Trioxocarbonates(IV) ($CO_3^{2-}$) All insoluble except those belonging to Sodium ($Na^+$), Potassium ($K^+$), and Ammonium ($NH_4^+$).

Introduction to Buffer Solutions

A Buffer solution is a chemical solution system that resists massive fluctuations in its $pH$ value when small increments of an acid or alkali are added to it.

  • Acidic Buffers: Composed of a weak acid paired with its conjugate salt (e.g., Ethanoic acid and Sodium ethanoate). They keep $pH$ windows anchored below 7.
  • Alkaline Buffers: Composed of a weak base paired with its conjugate salt (e.g., Ammonia solution and Ammonium chloride). They lock $pH$ windows above 7.
🔬 20-Year Laboratory Instruction: When executing qualitative separations, adding buffer arrays controls precipitate environments. For example, controlling iron or manganese precipitates requires sharp, buffered $pH$ environments to avoid overlapping readings!
