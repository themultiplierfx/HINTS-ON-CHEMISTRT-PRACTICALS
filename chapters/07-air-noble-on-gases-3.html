NOTE ON GASES 3

1. Practical Applications of Hydrogen Gas (H₂)

  • Oxy-hydrogen Flame: Used for melting metals because hydrogen is highly flammable and gives out a massive amount of heat when it burns.
  • Filling Balloons: Used historically because it has an extremely low density, providing great lifting force.

2. Industrial Manufacture of Hydrogen Gas

Hydrogen is produced commercially on a large scale using three primary pathways:

  1. By Bosch Process: Leverages red-hot coke and steam.
  2. From Methane: Uses hydrocarbons (like methane) instead of coke substrate.
  3. By Electrolytic Methods: Yields very pure hydrogen gas from brine (concentrated $\text{NaCl}$ solution).

3. The Mechanics of the Bosch Process

The classic Bosch process operates across two core reaction stages:

Stage I: Water Gas Production (Endothermic)

Steam is passed over red-hot coke at a high temperature of about $1200^\circ\text{C}$ to generate water gas:

C(s) + H₂O(g) → CO(g) + H₂(g)     [Water Gas Mixture]

Stage II: Catalytic Reduction of Steam (Exreversible-Exothermic)

Excess steam is mixed with the water gas at $450^\circ\text{C}$ in the presence of an iron oxide catalyst to convert carbon(II) oxide into carbon(IV) oxide, yielding extra hydrogen:

CO(g) + H₂(g) + H₂O(g) ≤→ CO₂(g) + 2H₂(g)

Catalyst Used: Iron(III) oxide ($\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3$) or Chromium(III) oxide ($\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_3$).

Finally, the newly generated $\text{CO}_2$ gas is separated out by dissolving it in water under high pressure, leaving pure hydrogen gas behind.

4. Water Gas vs. Producer Gas Dynamics

  • Water Gas: A gaseous fuel mixture of Carbon(II) oxide and Hydrogen ($\text{CO} + \text{H}_2$).
  • Producer Gas: A gaseous fuel mixture of Carbon(II) oxide and Nitrogen ($\text{CO} + \text{N}_2$).
🔬 Important Examination Distinction: Water gas burns much better than producer gas. Why? Because producer gas contains about $67\%$ non-combustible Nitrogen gas! Nitrogen does not support combustion and acts as a major diluent, slowing down the rate of combustion and structural corrosion. Hydrogen, however, strongly supports combustion.
